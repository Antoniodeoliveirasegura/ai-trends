Anthropic Publishes the First Public Scorecard for How Fast AI Is Building AI

Anthropic released three transparency metrics it says any frontier lab could adopt: Claude now "leads" — completes a task end-to-end from a high-level prompt while a human supervises — about 26% of the company's internal AI R&D work, up from effectively 0% six months ago; every action among its roughly 30,000 internal agents is screened before it runs; and about 6% of a sample week's research compute in July went to safety work.

It matters because this is the first time a frontier lab has quantified, rather than just gestured at, how much of the next model is being built by the last one — and it lands the same week that a former Anthropic/OpenAI researcher's resignation post ("racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives") hit 90M+ views and Dario Amodei published his own case for slowing down. Reaction is split between crediting Anthropic for disclosing at all and questioning whether "led, but supervised" work at 26% is meaningfully different from the autonomy everyone's actually worried about.

A Robot's "Muscle Memory" Cuts Vision-Language-Action Inference Time by 42%

rMuscle notices that robot task executions repeat far more than they look — not just in what the robot sees and does, but in the model's internal activations — and caches both to skip redundant computation. Result: a 1.42x speedup on consumer (RTX 4090) and edge (Jetson Thor) hardware across three manipulation benchmarks and real robots, with no loss in success rate — the kind of unglamorous efficiency win that matters more for deployed robotics than another benchmark record.

Quiet but Worth Watching

A Two-Line Fix Could Make Cheap, Flaky GPUs Usable for RL Training

Together AI researchers Martin Marek and Max Ryabinin traced most of the instability in training LLMs with reinforcement learning under quantization or laggy "stale" rollouts to one fixable source — a mismatch between the sampler's and trainer's token scores — and found that simply subtracting the sampler's expected score cancels it out. Tested from 0.6B to 30B parameters, "score centering" matches or beats more complex correction methods, with the gap widening under exactly the cheap, distributed conditions that make RL training affordable in the first place. Nobody's tweeting about it, but it's the kind of paper that quietly lowers everyone else's training bill.
