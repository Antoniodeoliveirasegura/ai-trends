Lead Story

OpenAI Cuts Off Cursor's Model Access After SpaceX's Takeover, Citing "Trust"

OpenAI announced it is ending its partnership with Cursor, saying direct access to its models inside the coding tool will stop on November 12 — a response to Cursor parent Anysphere's roughly $60B acquisition by Elon Musk's SpaceX earlier this month. OpenAI framed the decision bluntly as "trust," pointing to Musk's track record with Twitter/X and xAI as reason to doubt SpaceX would keep the technology within its terms of service; Cursor's CEO says OpenAI models account for only about 5% of its traffic, and the companies say they're still discussing the decision.

It matters because it's the first time a frontier lab has publicly severed model access over who owns a downstream customer, turning AI model access into a geopolitical-style lever rather than a straightforward commercial contract. It also handed Anthropic an opening — the company said Cursor has been a trusted partner since Sonnet 3.5 and pledged more compute to backfill the gap — and set X ablaze with developers debating whether any AI-tool integration is now hostage to its parent company's rivalries.

Z.ai Finally Releases GLM-5.3 Weights After a Two-Week Hacking Scare

Z.ai released the open weights for GLM-5.3 on August 28, after holding them back for the first time in the model's history because the model topped CyberGym's vulnerability-discovery benchmark at 84.5% — ahead of Claude Mythos 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol — surfacing 2,436 vulnerabilities (1,097 critical or high) across 269 open-source projects, including flaws in the Linux kernel, VMware, and Apache code. Z.ai says the model began chaining multi-stage exploitation plans it wasn't explicitly trained for, making the delay a rare case of a lab pausing an open-weight release over offensive-cyber capability rather than typical safety concerns.

A Third of Americans Now Ask a Chatbot About Their Health

A Pew Research survey of 3,488 US adults finds 34% now use AI chatbots for at least one health-related task — mostly quick health information (28%), figuring out what's causing symptoms (25%), and finding low-cost care info (22%) — with 47% calling the answers extremely or very helpful, but only 29% comfortable sharing personal health data with the tools. A companion Pew report released the same day found Americans are more likely to say chatbots hurt than help people using them for loneliness, depression, or stress — a split verdict on AI's growing role as an unlicensed health advisor.
