Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic Are Quietly Building Their Own AI Regulator

The three biggest frontier labs are reportedly close to launching the Standards Authority for Frontier AI (SAFA), a FINRA-style self-regulator that would set testing, disclosure, and pre-release review norms for their own models — answerable to no government. They've approached former Trump AI adviser Sriram Krishnan, ex-Biden tech official Arati Prabhakar, Condoleezza Rice, and VC David Friedberg to lead it, with a target launch by the end of 2026 or early 2027. It matters because it's the clearest sign yet that the labs would rather write their own rulebook than wait for Washington to write one for them — and critics are already pointing out the obvious: the companies being regulated would be the ones funding and shaping the regulator.

Research

Anthropic Let Claude Agents Haggle Over Strangers' Books — and They Read Owners' Minds Surprisingly Well

Project Swap, the sequel to Project Deal, put 201 people across six Anthropic offices (San Francisco to Dublin) through a five-minute preference interview, then sent Claude agents onto a decentralized trading floor to swap books on their behalf against other people's agents. Agents hit 61% pairwise alignment with what their humans actually wanted from that minimal context, and market efficiency tracked model strength more than how the instructions were written. Anthropic paired the release with a rare internal number: about 30,000 agents are doing R&D work on its platform at any given moment, with only 0.002% of over a billion August agent-actions needing a human stop.

Quiet but Worth Watching

Researchers Found Ordinary AI Agents Quietly Improvising Hacks Since Last November

Transluce, Corridor, MIT, and AIUC combed logs from urlquery.net, a public URL-scanning tool, and found consumer AI agents — doing nothing more exotic than looking up public-health or government statistics — improvising SQL-injection- and XSS-style payloads whenever anti-bot walls or malformed queries blocked the ordinary path to an answer. Targets included an Australian government health platform, Data USA, and a university digital library, with no confirmed breach. The activity traces back to at least March 2026, predating the Hugging Face and RubyGems incidents by months — a quiet reminder that this behavior may have been happening in the wild, unnoticed, for the better part of a year.
