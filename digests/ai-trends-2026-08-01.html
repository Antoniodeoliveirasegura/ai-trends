GCC Formally Rejects AI-Generated Code Over 15 Lines — a Line in the Sand for Core Toolchains

The GCC steering committee's July 29 policy rejects any "legally significant" contribution — set at roughly 15 lines of code or text, per the GNU Project's own maintainer guidelines — that's generated or derived from an LLM. Developers can still use LLMs for research, bug-hunting, and patch review internally, AI-generated test cases are exempt, and any AI-assisted contribution now needs an "Assisted-by:" commit tag, mirroring what the Linux kernel adopted earlier this year. Discussion was still very much alive on Hacker News and Slashdot into July 31.

Study Audits 3,249 Hidden System-Prompt Instructions Across 88 Commercial AI Products

"AISPA," posted to arXiv on July 31 by a group including Rishi Bommasani, Erik Brynjolfsson, and Alex Pentland, is a systematic audit of the system prompts that quietly govern commercial AI products but are almost never disclosed to users or regulators. Scoring 3,249 individual instructions across eight dimensions — from identity transparency to manipulation prevention — the team classified each as protective of users or actively problematic, giving "what is my AI actually being told to do" a real dataset for the first time.

EU AI Act's Transparency Rules Go Live Tomorrow — Every AI Product Sold in Europe Has to Say So

Starting August 2, Article 50 requires any AI system placed on the EU market to disclose when a user is dealing with a machine (unless obvious from context), and any system generating synthetic audio, image, video, or text must mark that output in a machine-readable, AI-detectable way. Products already on the market get a grace period to December 2, but the obligation is broad — it hits general-purpose AI too, not just the "high-risk" category that's been pushed over a year out.
